Reducing the variance in KL estimator

August 20, 2026 · Muyu He

Problem. The standard estimator (”k1”) of the KL divergence at each token position between target distribution \(p\) and reference distribution \(q\), ie. \(\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[\log (p(x)/q(x))]\) is estimated with \(\log (p(x)/q(x))\) sampling the actual token \(x\). But the variance of this estimator based over sampled tokens \(x\) is high, which lead to training instability.

Contributor of variance in the k1 estimator

Let \(r = p(x)/q(x), \delta = r - 1\). Then we can rewrite \(\log r\) assuming \(|\delta | \lt 1\) (ie. \(p\) and \(q\) are close) as:

$$ \begin{aligned} \log r &= \log (1+ \delta) \\ &= \log (1) + \int_{0}^{\delta} \frac{\partial \log (1+ t)}{\partial t} dt \\ &= \log (1) + \int_{0}^{\delta} \frac{1}{1+t} dt \\ &= \log (1) + \int_{0}^{\delta} (1- t + t^2 - t^3 + ...) dt \ \ \text{ if } |\delta| \lt 1 \\ &= \log (1) + \left[t- \frac{t^2}{2} + \frac{t^3}{3} - \frac{t^4}{4} + ...\right]_{0}^{\delta} \\ &= \left[t- \frac{t^2}{2} + \frac{t^3}{3} - \frac{t^4}{4} + ...\right]_{0}^{\delta} \\ &= \delta - \frac{\delta^2}{2} + \frac{\delta^3}{3} - \frac{\delta^4}{4} + ... \end{aligned} $$

The derivation uses the fact that the geometric series of \(1/(1+t)\) is \(1- t + t^2 - t^3 + ...\). If \(|\delta| \ll 1\) meaning \(p\) and \(q\) are close, the linear term \(\delta\) dominates. If \(|\delta|\) is not far away from 1, then it is not guaranteed that the linear term dominates, for example when \(\delta=0.9, \delta^2 = 0.81\).

Add a 0-expectation term \(1/r-1\)

The term \(1/r-1\) has an expectation over tokens of 0, so it can be added to the k1 estimator \(\log r\) without changing the expectation (”k3”):

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p} [\frac{1}{r}-1] &= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p} [\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}-1] \\ &= \sum_{x}p(x) (\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}-1) \\ &= \sum_{x}(q(x) - p(x)) \\ &= \sum_{x}q(x) - \sum_{x}p(x) \\ &= 1 - 1 = 0 \\ \therefore \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[\log r] &= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[\log r + \frac{1}{r} - 1] \end{aligned} $$

Geometric series of the 0-expectation term

If we expand \(1/r\) around 1, given \(|\delta | \lt 1\), we get:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{r} &= \frac{1}{1+\delta} = 1 - \delta + \delta^2 - \delta^3 + ... \\ \therefore \frac{1}{r} -1 &= - \delta + \delta^2 - \delta^3 + ... \end{aligned} $$

which has a negative linear term \(- \delta\) that can cancel with the positive linear term in the k1 estimator.

Cancelling the linear term

When we expand the new k3 estimator, we get:

$$ \begin{aligned} (\log r) + (\frac{1}{r}) - 1 &= (\delta - \frac{\delta^2}{2} + \frac{\delta^3}{3} - \frac{\delta^4}{4} + ...) + (- \delta + \delta^2 - \delta^3 + ...) \\ &= \frac{\delta^2}{2} - \frac{2 \delta^3}{3} + \frac{3 \delta^4}{4} - \frac{4 \delta^5}{5} + ... \end{aligned} $$

The resulting estimator has lower fluctuation when \(\delta\) is small. Since the formula has no linear term, when \(|\delta| \ll 1\), \(\delta^2/2\) dominates. So the fluctuation is smaller than if it has a linear term.

K3 estimator is non-negative

We can prove that k3 is nonnegative by first finding its global minimum given \(\delta\):

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{Let } f(\delta) &= \log (1+\delta) + (\frac{1}{1+\delta}) - 1 \\ \therefore f'(\delta) &= \frac{1}{1+\delta} + \frac{1}{(1+\delta)^2} - 1 \\ &= \frac{\delta}{(1+\delta)^2} \\ \because f'(\delta) &= \frac{\delta}{(1+\delta)^2} = \begin{cases} \lt 0 & \delta \lt 0 \\ 0 & \delta = 0 \\ \gt 0 & \delta \gt 0 \end{cases} \\ \therefore \delta &\text{ is the global minimum} \\ \therefore \text{min}f(\delta) &= 0 \end{aligned} $$

K3 estimator has lower fluctuation than K1 when \(|\delta| \ll 1\), but not lower variance overall

Since k1 value is \(O(\epsilon)\) and k3 value is \(O(\epsilon^2)\), k1’s variance is \(O(\epsilon^2)\) and k3’s variance is \(O(\epsilon^4)\). 1. At \(|\delta| \ll 1\), k3’s variance is dominated by the \(\delta^4\) term so smaller than k1’s dominating \(\delta^2\) term. However, 2. at \(|\delta|\lt1\) but not \(|\delta| \ll 1\), k3’s variance is not necessarily smaller even with a leading \(\delta^4\) term. This can be seen by decomposing k3’s variance into k1’s variance with an extra term:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{Var}(k3) &= \text{Var}(k1 + C) \\ &= \text{Var}(k1) + \text{Var}(C) + 2 \text{Cov}(k1, C) \\ \therefore \text{Var}(k3) - \text{Var}(k1) &= \text{Var}(C) + 2 \text{Cov}(k1, C) \end{aligned} $$

Since it can be shown that the right-hand side is not necessarily negative, we cannot claim that k3 has lower variance than k1.
